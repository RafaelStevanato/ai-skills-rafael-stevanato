## Performance is conversion: 31% better LCP → +8% sales (Vodafone A/B)

**Apply when:** founders separate "conversion work" from performance work.

**Do:**
- Treat web performance as a conversion lever, not an engineering nicety.
- Raise conversion before you open Figma.
- Treat LCP and web vitals as a conversion experiment.
- Run it with the same rigor as copy tests.
- Apply it first on paid traffic, where every bounce is paid for.

**Evidence:**
- Vodafone Italy A/B tested web-vitals optimization.
- LCP: 31% better than control.
- Offer, creative, screen: same in both variants.
- Volume: 100k clicks and 34k visits/day per variant, paid traffic.
- Result: +8% sales, +15% lead-to-visit, +11% cart-to-visit.

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-08-04](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2084589988843299217)

## Compliments aren't validation — "what's missing for you to PIX now?" is

**Apply when:** you collect praise but no revenue (everyone loves your idea, nobody bought).

**Do:**
- Treat compliments as free and worth nothing, like your freemium.
- Test validation with the simplest test: try to sell.
- Ask directly when you cannot close inside your ICP: "Falta o que pra você fazer o PIX agora?" ("What's missing for you to send the PIX instant payment right now?")
- Read the pause after the question.
- Classify the answer: the real objection (your build list) or excuses (the "loved it" was just politeness).
- Ask the follow-up: "If it had that, would you pay now?"
- Do 20 sales this way.
- Sell before building.
- Turn each doubt into a sales argument or an FAQ item.
- Design after, to scale what conversation already proved sells.

**Evidence:**
- Richard sold 20 subscriptions off a gray clickable wireframe.
- The QR code was not even real.
- Nobody said "paint the wireframe".
- Buyers asked for invoices and a second mechanic login.
- Those doubts became sales arguments and FAQ items.
- The gray was enough to prove the only thing that mattered: people with the pain and in a hurry to pay.

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-07-07](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2074539418266812580) and [@richardrx · 2026-06-24](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2069743203863962080)

## "Paint the button" is judging the last line of a long cascade

**Apply when:**
- You gather or act on LP feedback.
- You reach for button color first.

**Do:**
- Treat the visual layer of a landing page as the tip of a cascade that begins at the ICP.
- Never critique "make it more colorful" first. It critiques the *end* of a process as if it were the start.
- Work the cascade in this order: ICP → the buyer's **awareness level** (Eugene Schwartz's 5 stages) → what you say and how → visual positioning (tone → form, color, type, space).
- At low awareness: open on the problem.
- At low awareness: name the pain in the first fold.
- At low awareness: then show the mechanism and your fix.
- At higher awareness: go straight to your advantages vs. alternatives.
- Never jump to button color. You silently (and probably wrongly) answer who you sell to, their awareness, which pain, and what tone.

**Quote:** "They're looking at the end of a process and thinking it's the beginning."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-06-23](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2069469303464730988)

## Scaling cold traffic is an honesty test for your page

**Apply when:**
- Ad spend goes up but revenue barely moves.
- The conversion rate "drops" even though you fixed nothing.

**Do:**
- Treat conversion measured on warm audiences (existing followers) as inflated. They forgive the page's flaws.
- Use cold traffic to reveal how much of your rate is the page versus borrowed trust.
- Treat any baseline built on warm public as fiction, not a performance number.
- Read the gap as the page's real ceiling when you scale budget into cold traffic and the rate collapses.
- Fix the obvious page defects first.
- Judge media only after the page fixes.

**Evidence:**
- A founder tripled ad spend. Revenue rose only 11%.
- Warm-traffic conversion of ~3% (driven by Instagram followers) cratered to 0.5% on cold traffic.
- Fixing the obvious lifted it to 2% — 4x the real number.

**Quote:** "Scaling budget into cold traffic works like an honesty test: it shows how much of your conversion is your page and how much is borrowed trust."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-06-08](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2063972594223661127)

## Test hero comprehension with a stranger from your ICP

**Apply when:** a hero trades clarity for "creativity".
- "Revolutionize your workflow" does not say what it does.
- "The definitive productivity platform" does not say for whom.

**Do:**
- Run this usability test: show your LP hero to one person for 5 seconds.
- Take the hero away.
- Ask: "What does this product do? For whom?"
- Mark the hero as failed if they cannot answer, no matter how pretty it is.
- Expect the brain to ask three questions in those seconds, almost in order: what is this → is this for me → why should I care now.
- Cut decoding effort. Every second spent decoding what you do is a second closer to giving up (cognitive load).
- Pick clarity over cleverness at the top of the funnel, always.
- Build the hero from five elements:
  1. A headline saying what the product does, in the customer's language, not internal jargon.
  2. A subheadline nailing the ICP and the concrete result.
  3. Real visual proof: a screenshot of the product working, not a generic smiling illustration.
  4. A specific CTA.
  5. Immediate social proof (a logo or a number) above the fold.
- Write specific heroes: "Controle de comandas pra oficina mecânica" ("Work-order control for auto repair shops") beats "Software de gestão" ("Management software").
- Cover everything on the page except the hero.
- Check that a stranger from your ICP can say what you sell in 5 seconds.
- Read with "Pass the 5-second test — lead with the problem, not the feature" (2026-05-01): that post argues for a pain-led headline, this one for a what-it-does headline — both demand the what / for whom / why-now answers inside 5 seconds.

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-05-29](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2060409264683044995)

## Write the CTA microcopy, not the button color

**Apply when:** a technical founder A/B tests button colors for weeks over a 0.3% (non-significant) delta while the label still says "Sign up" with no click trigger.

**Do:**
- Write the CTA label before you touch button color or shape. The label moves conversion far more.
- Treat the label as the last thing the user reads before deciding.
- Reduce the mental effort of simulating what happens next.
- Make the CTA answer the three questions the brain asks before clicking: (1) what happens when I click, (2) how long it takes, (3) what it costs/commits.
- Never use "Sign up". It answers none of the three.
- Use "Start free in 30s". It answers two.
- Add a click trigger (the small line under the CTA) to answer the rest and break an objection: "Start Free Trial" + "14 days, no card".
- Root the copy in outcome bias and Construal Level Theory (making the outcome concrete).

**Quote:** "The button is the last thing the user reads before deciding — treat it as such."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-05-25](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2058875777739866490)

## Cut CAC by filtering on the landing page, not the media

**Apply when:** clicks become leads, leads become SDR calls, calls don't close, and founders react by swapping creative or channel (or blaming the SDR).

**Do:**
- Treat high CAC as a filtering problem first. It is rarely a media problem.
- Qualify leads on the landing page before the form. This kills deals that were never going to close.
- Pull three filtering levers so the lead self-selects without feeling filtered:
  1. Copy specificity: "Cash flow for service providers billing R$500k–R$3M" beats "Organize your finances".
  2. Visible pricing: it screens out no-budget leads, so sales only meets real objections.
  3. A qualification question as the first interaction (or copy/examples that play that role).
- Measure CAC as cost per closed deal, not cost per lead.

**Evidence:** If you close 1 in 50 leads, dropping to 30 leads with 1 close nearly doubles media efficiency — same spend, lower CAC.

**Quote:** "The lead with no money won't magically generate money between the landing page and the sales call."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-05-18](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2056385204785213446)

## Aim for 4.2–4.5 stars, not a perfect 5

**Apply when:** you build or curate reviews, ratings, and social proof on a landing page or product.

**Do:**
- Never show a flawless rating. It reads as fake, and consumers distrust unanimity.
- Include constructive criticism in the profile. It feels more authentic and converts better than pure praise.
- Let imperfection show.
- Stack the highest-converting combination: a detailed case study with quantifiable ROI.
- Add a video testimonial from a brand recognizable to your ICP, ideally.
- Add customer logos.
- Ban the common failure, social proof done badly: identical cards, first-name-only, all 5 stars, generic avatars instead of a real photo.
- Make each review verifiable (e.g., link to the actual LinkedIn post).

**Evidence:**
- Northwestern: purchase probability peaks at 4.2–4.5 stars, not 5.
- Testimonials lift LP conversion up to 34% (VWO).
- 5+ reviews → 270% more likely to be bought (Yotpo).
- 93% read reviews before buying (BrightLocal).

**Visual:** A social-proof wall done right: real faces, names + roles, verifiable reviews, and recognizable client logos (Volkswagen, Coca-Cola, Samsung, Itaú, Volvo, DocuSign, Hotmart) under a "+10,000 professionals" headline — `../assets/2054910531132183030__1.jpg`

**Quote:** "Credibility drives conversion."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-05-14](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2054910531132183030)

## Compress the decision window — shorten the path to action below the path to doubt

**Apply when:** you optimize the checkout, CTA, or headline while you ignore the deliberation time upstream.

**Do:**
- Assume the purchase decision is mostly made before checkout.
- Treat conversion as architecture: the user converts when the path to the action is shorter than the path to doubt.
- Cut deliberation time. Every extra second raises the odds the user closes the tab.
- Compress decision time in one of two ways.
- Way 1: pressurize the environment with real scarcity ("Only 1 room left at this price", "Booked 3 times in the last hour").
- Way 1 works through loss aversion (Kahneman: losing hurts up to ~2x more than the equivalent gain), so "1 left" registers as "I'll lose this".
- Way 2: remove steps entirely (Amazon's patented 1-Click ordering).

**Evidence:**
- Booking runs 1,000+ simultaneous experiments (per Lukas Vermeer, 8 yrs leading experimentation).
- Showing sold-out hotels alongside available ones *raised* bookings by sharpening perceived scarcity.

**Quote:** "The user converts when the path to the action is shorter than the path to doubt."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-05-11](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2053832356293738914)

## Don't clone the page — the converting layer is invisible

**Apply when:** a page "looks like it works" and you are tempted to clone it for your own offer.

**Do:**
- Treat a converting sales page as the visible shell of an invisible system.
- Never copy the layout. It copies what is cheapest to produce, and the expensive part (research) stays invisible and gets left behind.
- Ask before copying: what on this page is a function of the *product*?
- Ask before copying: what is a function of the *customer research* done before writing each line and placing each element?
- Look for the hidden layer:
  - Customer vocabulary pulled from interviews.
  - Objections ordered to the ICP's specific fear.
  - Social proof hand-picked to resonate.
  - A core promise tuned to the stated desire.
- Name the error: cargo cult. It replicates the visible ritual hoping to summon the result, without grasping the causal mechanism.

**Evidence:**
- Two ~95%-identical pages, same niche/offer/order: 4% vs 0.6%.
- The 4% page belonged to Richard's client.
- The 0.6% page was a near-pixel clone by a builder.
- The builder unknowingly DM'd the original team to complain it wasn't converting.

**Quote:** "There's a name for copying the form expecting the function. It's called cargo cult."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-05-09](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2053086365781205142)

## Pass the 5-second test — lead with the problem, not the feature

**Apply when:** your hero opens by describing what the product *is/does* (a feature/category) instead of the visitor's pain.

**Do:**
- Assume a visitor scans your LP for ~5 seconds.
- Make the hero answer three things inside those seconds: what it does, who it's for, why they should care.
- Expect visitors to leave when they cannot answer. They will not stay to figure it out.
- Start conversion on the first line.
- Open with the visitor's problem, not yourself.
- Replace the first line, as in this pair:
  - Before: "AI-powered project management platform" → meh
  - After: "Your team loses 6 hours a week hunting for information" → keep going
- Write about the visitor. One line talks about itself, the other talks about me.
- Connect the first line with the pain. If it doesn't, the rest of the page goes invisible.

**Quote:** "If you who built it can't answer in 5 seconds, your visitor can't either."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-05-01](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2050280273682510230)

## Fix contrast, layout, and trust before rewriting copy

**Apply when:** you rewrite home copy for weeks chasing conversion without touching the visual/attentional layer.

**Do:**
- Work three pre-verbal variables before you rewrite copy: contrast, on-screen placement, and trust.
- Expect the brain to process them before it reads the words.
- Expect them to move the needle more than any headline, usually.
- Contrast: set it relative to surroundings, not as an isolated color.
- Contrast: use a big, differently-shaped button in whitespace. It is *seen before it's read*.
- Contrast: treat the accessibility ratio 4.5:1 as a floor, not the goal.
- Placement: apply Fitts's law (closer, bigger targets get clicked more).
- Placement: apply the F-pattern (Nielsen Norman eyetracking).
- Placement: never put a CTA bottom-right with no visual anchor. It sits outside the attentional map.
- Trust: build it in four reinforcing layers:
  1. A real (non-stock) human face.
  2. A known brand or specific verifiable proof.
  3. An offer that fits the stated problem.
  4. A solution mechanism logically coherent with the promise.
- Align all four layers. When all four align, the user converts without knowing why.
- Fix any failing layer. When one fails, the user invents a rational reason to leave.

**Quote:** "Optimizing copy without working contrast, placement, and trust is masking the symptom."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-04-28](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2049215050997784774)

## Specificity is the difference between decoration and persuasion

**Apply when:** your hero reads "The complete platform for [generic category]", a 5-feature subtitle, "Start free", and no real social proof — the default Brazilian SaaS template.

**Do:**
- Write specific copy. The brain discards generic LP copy.
- Expect specific messages to be processed faster and to generate more trust.
- Show the *transformation* the product causes, not just what it does.
- Fix the four standard failures with these before → after pairs:

| Failure | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Vague claim | "Complete platform" means nothing | "Cut new-dev onboarding time 40%" |
| Features on top | features on top | lead with the pain, feature as the solution |
| Weakest CTA | "Start free" (no value, no risk reduction, no urgency) | "See your first report in 2 minutes" |
| Empty proof | "Used by Company X" proves nothing | "We cut Company X's churn from 12% to 6%" |

- Lead with the pain. Nobody wakes up wanting a "custom report feature".
- Build the page that sells, not the page that describes.

**Evidence:** Across 30 Brazilian SaaS LPs: descriptive vs transformation-led ran 0.5% vs 3% conversion — same traffic, 6x more leads — driven by argument sequence and promise specificity, not visual design.

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-04-17](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2045154631974539650)

## Users scan, they don't read — give the primary action obvious contrast

**Apply when:**
- The main CTA competes with 6 other elements.
- The most important action is buried inside a dropdown that requires a click to reveal.

**Do:**
- Design for scanning. Users scan interfaces, weigh cognitive effort against payoff, and ignore most of it.
- Expect one question on entry: "What can I do here?"
- Make the primary action win on contrast within seconds.
- Make the primary action visually dominant so users find it at a glance.
- Never let the key button compete with too many elements. It loses prominence.
- Never hide the key action behind interaction. It is effectively invisible.
- Leverage image superiority to guide attention down the page.

**Visual:** A login screen annotated with two biases — "limited choice bias" (a single dominant Google sign-in button) and "image superiority" (a vivid hero illustration pulling the eye) — `../assets/2042013751742705816__1.jpg`

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-04-08](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2042013751742705816)

## Judge a landing page by conversion, not by beauty

**Apply when:** the timeline turns into "AI vs human" / "who designed it better" debates that ignore conversion, while obvious conversion flaws go unaddressed.

**Do:**
- Never treat a landing page as a beauty contest.
- Judge a landing page by its one measurable job: qualify the user and lower CAC by turning traffic into revenue.
- Never evaluate a static image with no context. It is nearly useless.
- Treat conversion as a continuous loop, never an isolated event:
  1. Form a thesis.
  2. Ship the interface and collect data.
  3. Find where the user hesitated.
  4. Form a new hypothesis.
  5. Optimize the next cycle.
- Expect debate to default to aesthetics, because it is easier to opine on looks than to measure results.
- Know that this default keeps most LPs underperforming.

**Visual:** A side-by-side "Google Stitch vs Human" LP comparison being judged on looks alone — context for the argument, not a model to copy — `../assets/2034638793219694734__q__1.jpg`

**Quote:** "Product design doesn't compete with art. It sells."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-03-19](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2034638793219694734)

## Converting pages are often ugly — design for performance, not applause

**Apply when:** you choose between a page that wins design compliments and one built to convert.

**Do:**
- Expect an invisible war between branding (looking good in the screenshot) and performance (conversion, retention, expansion).
- Expect the page that puts money in the till to be often visually aggressive, text-heavy, and far from the Apple aesthetic founders dream of.
- Engineer the converting pattern even if the artist in you cringes:
  1. One obvious attention point: eyes go where they must.
  2. A high-contrast CTA with redundancy.
  3. A clear promise delivering easily-perceived value.
  4. Guarantee, social proof, and a free element to reduce risk aversion.
  5. Organic images by the second fold to back the offer and build trust.

**Evidence:** The analyzed page (quoted): 100 visits → 19 signups, a 19% conversion rate.

## Design the features page for skimming, not reading

**Apply when:** you pack a features page with dense prose and expect visitors to read it top to bottom.

**Do:**
- Design for skimming. Almost nobody reads your features page.
- Expect visitors to skim three bullets and hunt for a demo video.
- Front-load three crisp bullets.
- Make a demo video easy to find.
- Write the deep copy for the minority of technical, high-awareness buyers who actually read it.
- Keep the caveat: the more conscious, technical slice of your ICP *will* read the detail.

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-02-09](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2020815036168437814)

## No clicks out, plus brutal CTA contrast

**Apply when:**
- You are tempted to link out to a glowing NY Times piece or a top influencer's video.
- Your CTA blends into the interface.

**Do:**
- Keep distraction low and redundancy high for conversion.
- Treat any clickable element that leads off-page as lost conversion, no matter how prestigious the source.
- Kill outbound clicks entirely.
- Make the CTA generate strong contrast against the rest of the interface in position, size, and color.
- Never ship a CTA that is hard to notice and click. It will not be clicked.

## Debug ICP and awareness level before touching design

**Apply when:**
- Your LP underperforms and you reach for button-color tweaks.
- You define ICP by demographics.

**Do:**
- Treat conversion as context engineering: who arrives, with what pain, at what stage, needing what proof.
- Debug two invisible variables before you write a line of code: the Who (ICP) and the When (awareness level).
- Leave visual design for last.
- Reject the demographic fallacy. Geography, age, and income are a bad proxy.
- Remember the proof: King Charles and Ozzy Osbourne are demographic twins with opposite needs.
- Define the real ICP by *buying criteria*: trigger, pain, prior attempt, proof needed.
- Treat persona as biography only, the spec for your user.
- Map the visitor's Eugene Schwartz awareness level.
- Write the headline to the current stage.
- Use proof to push the visitor one stage forward.
- Never sell "Solution" to the "Unaware". It is proposing on a first date.
- Debug in this order: ICP wrong → awareness wrong → proof insufficient for the stage → only then touch visual design.
- Use this table for awareness → angle / hero / proof:

| Awareness | Angle | Hero | Proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | symptom & identity | "Still doing X this way?" | simple diagnostic, checklist, benchmark |
| **Problem-aware** | cost & urgency | "If you have X, you're losing Y" | numbers, before/after, calculation |
| **Solution-aware** | trade-offs & selection | "3 ways to solve X — why the 3rd scales" | honest comparison, matrix |
| **Product-aware** | differentiation & proof stack | "Why us, why now" | cases, demo, objections |
| **Most aware** | final risk & friction | "Swap X for Y in Z days, no risk" | guarantees, onboarding, effort reduction |

**Visual:**
- Schwartz's 5 awareness stages as a rising ramp (Unaware → Problem → Solution → Product → Most Aware) — `../assets/2011427153133351046__3.jpg`
- The demographic-twins diagram (Charles vs Ozzy, identical on paper) — `../assets/2011427153133351046__2.jpg`

**Quote:** "There's no 'conversion rate' in a vacuum. There's contextualized conversion."

## The 2026 landing page checklist

**Apply when:** you audit or build any LP.

**Do:**
- Use the all-in-one checklist for a converting LP from @namyakhann (Supafast).
- Know that Richard translated the list and endorsed it in full.
- Run the checklist top to bottom.

Hero (5):
- [ ] State 1 clear promise.
- [ ] Target 1 specific audience.
- [ ] Use 1 exact-action CTA.
- [ ] Show, don't explain, in the visual.
- [ ] Place a trust seal above the fold.

Copy (5):
- [ ] Write a headline <10 words.
- [ ] Put benefit before feature.
- [ ] Use customer language.
- [ ] Sell one central transformation.
- [ ] Make the page scannable in 5s.

Social proof (4):
- [ ] Show logos if you have names.
- [ ] Show metrics if you have traction.
- [ ] Use specific result testimonials.
- [ ] Use zero generic praise.

Structure (4):
- [ ] Order sections: Hero → Pain → Solution → Proof → CTA.
- [ ] Set max 3 feature sections.
- [ ] Write the FAQ to kill top 5 objections.
- [ ] Repeat the CTA 2–3x.

Technical (3):
- [ ] Load in <3s.
- [ ] Build real mobile-first.
- [ ] Keep a single-scroll flow.

**Mantra:** Clarity > creativity. Result > feature. Speed > polish. If it doesn't convert in 2026, it's not your traffic — it's your page.

**Conflict note (headline length):**
- The Supafast hero formula in [ai-era-differentiation.md](ai-era-differentiation.md) ("Sell the value, not the feature list — and show the product") says `Headline (≤8 words, attack the #1 pain)`.
- This checklist says headline <10 words.
- Apply the stricter rule: keep the headline at 8 words or fewer.
- Treat <10 words as the outer bound only.

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-01-02](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2007216871607771303)

## Click triggers: one line of risk-reducing microcopy next to the CTA

**Apply when:** users hesitate right before the CTA.

**Do:**
- Add a single line of text next to the CTA that reduces perceived risk. It can save conversion.
- Call it a "click trigger" in CRO. Marketing calls it objection-breaking.
- Write click triggers like these: "Lifetime guarantee," "Free shipping," "7 days free, no card."
- Place certainty on top of a known ICP doubt.
- Find the real objections by talking to customers.
- Read complaint pages (ReclameAqui) and app-store comments.
- Test which click trigger performs best.

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2025-12-19](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2002076748112875558)

## Founder LP guide: platform doesn't matter, load time does; proof must outweigh the promise

**Apply when:** you build a first LP cheaply.

**Do:**
- Know that profits are more sensitive to conversions than to costs.
- Treat the landing page as often the first gate between a founder's product and its users.
- Rank speed, clarity, contrast, proof, and direct action above the platform.
- Follow five rules:
  1. Use any platform, but deliver content fast and target an "A" on GTMetrix.
  2. Lead with a short pain or clear benefit, then make the promise tangible in the subhead.
  3. Create visual contrast with what the ICP sees daily.
  4. Ensure proof ≥ promise, moving from strong/shallow at the top to denser evidence below.
  5. Use direct CTAs tied to the outcome about every 1.5 sections.

**Quote:** "Nobody wants to know about your product's features — they want what it generates or the pain it kills. Not 6GB of space, 3,000 songs in your pocket."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2025-11-10](https://x.com/richardrx/status/1987842649261261240)

## Match CTA microcopy to the funnel stage

**Apply when:** one generic CTA label serves the whole journey.

**Do:**
- Change button text with the user's progress in the funnel.
- Match the verb to the mental state at each stage.
- Use these labels:

| Funnel stage | CTA label | English gloss |
|---|---|---|
| First interaction | "Descubra como funciona" | "See how it works" |
| Product page | "Quero esse produto" | "I want this product" |
| Checkout | "Finalizar minha compra" | "Complete my purchase" |

- Keep each label short (≤4 words), clear, and directional.
- Expect this discreet personalization to raise perceived control, confidence, and conversion.
- Audit every CTA against the stage it appears in.
- Test stage-matched variants.

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2025-11-07](https://x.com/richardrx/status/1986836679953719632)

## Real humans outconvert models — banner blindness explains why

**Apply when:** you choose imagery for LPs and proof sections.

**Do:**
- Prefer real users and the real team over polished models.
- Prefer user-generated reviews and simple real photos over studio shots. They often win.
- Expect banner blindness to explain the effect (likely explanation): people learn to ignore what looks like advertising.
- Expect people to give attention to what is organic or unusual.
- Note that Chinese sites exploit this at scale with gamified UGC.

**Evidence:**
- Highrise, the 37signals CRM, tested homepage variants with different human figures.
