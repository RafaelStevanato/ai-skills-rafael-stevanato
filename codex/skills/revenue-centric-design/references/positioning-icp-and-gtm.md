# Positioning, ICP & Go-to-Market

## Size the market with TAM → SAM → SOM (and know which one matters)

**Principle:** Your real market is far smaller than the population. Investors often ignore a TAM under ~R$1B. The number that matters is the **SOM**: what you can actually capture.

**Apply when:** You size a market, write a deck or judge whether a niche is big enough.

**Do:**
- Define TAM as the total addressable ceiling: a theoretical number (Brazil's 213M becomes ~101M credit-card holders for a paid app).
- Define **SAM** as the realistic slice your model reaches (~40% in his example).
- Define **SOM** as the 1–5% you truly win in ~36 months. Derive it from real CAC, activation, support capacity and LTV, never a guessed %.
- Do not try to change your TAM. Change how much of your SAM you convert and retain.

**Evidence:** RepareCar: ~76k mechanic shops (honest TAM) → SAM ~47k → ~3% ≈ 1,414 shops ≈ R$1.6M ARR. Current pace (~10 shops/day) ≈ 7% of SAM in 12 months.

## Frame the referral prize as a gift to the friend, not a commission to the referrer

**Principle:** Member-get-member (MGM) referral programs win on framing and timing, not only on a two-sided reward. Money makes the exchange feel transactional. An in-product benefit feels like a genuine gift.

**Apply when:** You design or fix a referral program and default to "refer a friend, get $20."

**Do:**
- Apply the framing effect: surface the prize on the receiver's side ("João gave you 500MB").
- Ask for the referral at the peak of value: right after a concrete win, or when the user hits a limit.
- Avoid cash. Give a reward that deepens use of your own product.
- Embed the referral as continuous in-product operation, not a one-off campaign.

**Evidence:** Dropbox grew 3900% in 15 months (100k → 4M users), peaking near 3M invites in a single month. ~1/3 of users already arrived via word-of-mouth before the program.

**Quote:** "A referral amplifies a product people already love — it doesn't fix a product nobody recommends for free."

## Pick a deliberately under-served niche as your ICP

**Principle:** A clear ICP (ideal customer profile) is not "everyone who could use my product." A sharp niche beats no niche: you cannot out-fight the entrenched generalist giant.

**Apply when:** You have early traction and feel tempted to "embrace the world" out of fear of a small TAM.

**Do:**
- Validate four ICP filters:
  1. The segment feels the pain with real weight. Pain is proportional to what is lost when the problem stays unsolved: a lost lead costs a face-aesthetics clinic R$3,000 vs. R$60 for a barber.
  2. The TAM is big enough to sustain operations.
  3. The segment has money to pay your required ticket, so unit economics close.
  4. Founder-fit: it gives native language, a fast validation network, and instinct that money can't buy.
- With a clear ICP, failure has a diagnosis ("I got the messaging wrong"). Without one, you cannot tell if product, copy, channel, price or audience failed, and every test burns runway.

**Quote:** "A generalist ERP is hard to sell; an ERP for cabinetmaking is a different conversation."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-05-19](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2056789797646029232)

## Don't claim PLG without the four structural conditions

**Principle:** Product-led growth (PLG) is a consequence of structural conditions, not a product decision you declare. Most B2B SaaS that pitches PLG is really sales-led wearing a PLG label.

**Apply when:** You write a pitch deck or strategy and call the motion "self-service" / PLG.

**Do:**
- Require all four conditions before you claim PLG:
  1. TTV < 10 minutes. A consultant demo, API support or paid implementation means it is not PLG. Tell: full trial, zero activation.
  2. Ticket below ~R$1,000. A higher ticket means a buying committee.
  3. Native virality or collaboration. Notion, Figma and Slack pull users in. A CRM/AI tool needs SDRs, demos, follow-up.
  4. A huge addressable market with a real bottom-up TAM.
- If you fail these conditions, run sales-led honestly.

**Evidence:** Brazil has ~20,000 companies with 100+ employees, and only ~a dozen B2B SaaS where PLG makes real economic sense.

**Quote:** "Founders love PLG because it seems to delete the part they don't master — selling."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-05-04](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2051262752547536941)

## Charge your first ten users from day one

**Principle:** The first ten users define the product's entire curve. Payment is the cheapest test of real pain.

**Apply when:** You validate a new product and feel tempted to give early access away to "build a base."

**Do:**
- Source the first ten from closed communities, personal reach, or pure guerrilla.
- Charge even while the product is a prototype.
- When someone says they can't pay, ask: "What does the system need to do for you to pay right now?" Treat the payment friction as part of the test.
- Collect dense feedback. Start visual design only after ~20 paying users.
- For B2C apps, shift the method (e.g., pre-sale of a solution-in-progress). Keep the principle.

**Evidence:** RepareCar's first 25 auto shops tested the product in prototype. The team visited each shop, charged at the end, and designed visuals only after 20 paying shops.

**Quote:** "Curiosity is free; an open wallet demands a concrete problem."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-04-26](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2048359526487716333)

## Reverse-engineer the funnel math before celebrating an MRR target

**Principle:** Treat a revenue target as a traffic-and-retention problem. Churn quietly resets the whole funnel every month.

**Apply when:** Someone asks "is it hard to hit X MRR?" or you size acquisition for a target customer count.

**Do:**
- Work backwards from the target. To net 2,500 customers at 5% LP conversion, you need 50,000 visitors. From ads at 3% creative CTR, you need ~1.6M impressions.
- Treat 5% and 3% as top-decile. Most land at 1–2% LP and under 1% CTR, so test dozens.
- Add the leaky bucket. At 20% monthly churn, you replace 500 customers every month forever just to stand still (≈10,000 visitors / 333,000 impressions).
- Assign the problem to dev, design and marketing together. No single function owns it.

**Evidence:** 20% monthly churn → 5-month average lifetime. At low ticket, many operate at 40–50% churn, so "the bucket never fills."

**Quote:** "Building the product is the easy part; distribution is the game."

**Source:** [@richardrx · 2026-04-20](https://x.com/richardrx/status/2046222319912132977)

## Concentrate channels with the Bullseye framework, not scattershot testing

**Principle:** Testing ten channels at once hides what drove results, and you blame the channel when the business stalls. Prioritize distribution with focus.

**Apply when:** You spread content and traffic across many channels with no clear read on what works.

**Do:**
- Use the Bullseye framework (from the book *Traction*): three rings of priority.
  - Inner ring: at most three highest-potential channels, with total focus.
  - Middle ring: up to six channels you probe with small experiments.
  - Outer ring: everything plausible long-term, no active focus now.
- Choose between channels with an ICE Score (Impact, Confidence, Ease, each 0–10, divide by 3, prioritize).
- Close the gap the book skips: scaling distribution onto a broken reception structure (LP, onboarding, first product steps) yields no growth. Run distribution and retention simultaneously, not sequentially.

## Diagnose the bottleneck: no entries is distribution, leaving without paying is design

**Principle:** Design cannot save a "ghost product." Design optimizes and raises the LTV of something that already has traffic. It cannot manufacture demand.

**Apply when:** A builder ships an app, gets near-zero users, and hopes a redesign will rescue it.

**Do:**
- Split the diagnosis. If nobody enters your product, it is a distribution problem. If users enter, don't pay, and leave, it is a design problem.
- Read *Traction*, even if you can afford an agency or a marketing team.
- Check each channel's awareness level, not only cost-per-channel. Awareness level drives different conversion and retention behavior, depending on where and how the user arrived.

**Quote:** "If nobody enters your product, it's distribution. If they enter, don't pay, and leave, that's design."
